    Trends and drift¶

    Why it matters¶

    Many features describe fluctuations about a stable level: autocorrelations and correlation times, the shape of the spectrum, forecasting errors, symbolic and entropy measures that split the series at its mean. A trend leaks into all of them. Three mechanisms do most of the work. The z-score denominator includes the trend’s variance, so the fluctuations are shrunk; features that set their own delay from the autocorrelation time read a trended series as slow and choose long delays; and features that split the series at its mean or into global halves see the two ends of a trended series as different regimes. In a screen of the default feature set on synthetic series, a linear rise of three standard deviations over the record moved about half the features by more than the realisation-to-realisation spread; a rise of one standard deviation moved almost nothing.

    Other features measure the trend on purpose: trend(), monotonic_trend() (the Mann-Kendall tau and the Theil-Sen slope), fit_polynomial(), the drifting-mean and stationarity tests, and every *_raw feature computed without z-scoring. These report what they should, and detrending the data would make them uninformative.

    What pyhctsa does internally¶

    Nothing is detrended inside the feature library. Every feature is computed on the series as given (z-scored where its configuration says so), so that its name says what it computes on every series alike.

    A small family of *_lindt features gives the linear correlation structure of the linearly detrended series alongside the originals: the autocorrelation at every configured lag (ac_<tau>_lindt), the partial autocorrelation function (partial_autocorr_20_burg_lindt), the first zero and 1/e crossing times of the autocorrelation function (first_crossing_ac_0_both_lindt, first_crossing_ac_1/e_both_lindt), the shape of its decay (autocorr_shape_drown_lindt, autocorr_shape_pos_drown_lindt) and the histogram automutual information at lags 1 to 5 (histogram_ami_*_lindt). Each is the same operation applied to zscore(detrend_linear(x_z)): the least-squares line through the z-scored series is removed and the residual z-scored again (detrend_linear(), the preprocess: detrend setting of a feature configuration). The *_lindt form is exactly invariant to an added line and otherwise differs from the original only by what the fitted line absorbs. A series that is a line up to round-off has no residual to work with, so its *_lindt features are NaN.

    Keep two limits in mind. Only a line is removed: a curved drift, a step or a slow oscillation is removed only in part (a step of two standard deviations in the middle of the record still moves the *_lindt features, by about a third of what it does to the originals). And a line fitted to a long-memory series absorbs some of its real low-frequency power, so the *_lindt autocorrelations of such a series sit a little below the originals: for a fractionally integrated series with d = 0.4 and 1000 samples, by about half the realisation-to-realisation spread at every lag. The features that set a delay from the autocorrelation time still use the raw series’ timescale.

    Recommendation¶

    When the series in a dataset are trended, decide once what to remove and apply it to every series, so that the features compare series on the same footing. Never detrend a subset of the series: a feature then means different things for different rows.

    Look at the trend before deciding. The features that measure it (trend, monotonic_trend, fit_polynomial, the KPSS test) are themselves a summary: on a z-scored series trend.stdRatio is the fraction of the standard deviation that survives a linear detrend, fit_polynomial_1 to fit_polynomial_3 say how much a line, a quadratic and a cubic leave, and monotonic_trend.tau says how consistently the series rises or falls. A trend that a line explains is removed by detrend_linear(); a curved drift or a slow oscillation needs a method of your own (a polynomial, a high-pass filter), applied to every series alike; a random walk is made stationary by differencing (numpy.diff, or the *_diff1 features), not by detrending.

    from pyhctsa.calculator import FeatureCalculator
from pyhctsa.utils import detrend_linear

data_lindt = [detrend_linear(x) for x in data]   # the least-squares line removed from every series
res = FeatureCalculator().extract(data_lindt)

    After this the *_lindt features equal their originals, and the features that measure trend report nothing: drop both from the result, or keep the trend measurements from a run on the original data if they matter to your question. When the trend is itself the signal of interest (a growth rate, a drift between conditions), do not detrend; the trend measurements and the *_raw features carry it.
